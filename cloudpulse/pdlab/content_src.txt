
CH|1|INTRODUCTION
P|Effective cloud cost management has become an urgent concern as organisations, start-ups and students move their workloads to public clouds. Platforms such as Microsoft Azure and Amazon Web Services bill by the second or the hour, so every virtual machine, disk and network address that is created and then forgotten keeps costing money. CloudPulse is a cloud cost monitoring and optimisation platform that finds this wasted spend, assigns each problem to the person who created the resource, tracks the fix and proves the saving on the next bill. This chapter presents the background of the problem, the purpose of the platform, the objectives and scope of the project, and the way the rest of the report is organised.
H1|1.1|BACKGROUND
P|Cloud computing changed how technology is bought. Instead of purchasing servers in advance, a team can create a virtual machine, a database or a storage account in minutes and pay only for what it uses [1], [2]. This elasticity is the main strength of the cloud and also its main financial risk, because resources are easy to create and easy to forget. A virtual machine that was shut down from inside its operating system is reported as stopped, yet the platform still reserves its hardware and keeps billing for compute. A data disk that was detached when a project ended continues to be charged for every gigabyte it holds, and static public IP addresses and snapshots add a little more every day. None of these items is large on its own, but across hundreds of resources and many teams they add up to a monthly figure that delivers no value.
P|The discipline that deals with this problem is called FinOps, or cloud financial operations. The FinOps Foundation describes it as a practice in which engineering, finance and business teams share responsibility for cloud spend and make timely, data-driven decisions [3]. A central idea of FinOps is that visibility alone is not enough: a cost problem is solved only when somebody who owns it acts and the saving can be shown on the next bill. Cloud providers publish tools for the first step, visibility, and publish pricing and audit data that make the later steps possible, but the steps are rarely joined together in one product that a small team or a student can understand and trust.
P|The problem is felt most sharply by students and small teams. A student who experiments on Azure with the free credit of Azure for Students can exhaust that credit with a handful of forgotten resources and has no tool that explains why. A small team needs to keep a monthly bill predictable, an engineering manager needs to know which team owns a wasteful resource, and finance needs savings that are real rather than estimated. CloudPulse was designed for these people: it explains each figure, it asks for nothing more than read-only access, and it shows its evidence.
H1|1.2|PURPOSE OF CLOUDPULSE
P|The purpose of CloudPulse is to carry every cost problem through the same loop, from the first signal to a verified result. Native consoles such as Azure Cost Management are very good at answering how much was spent and on what. They are weaker at answering which individual resources are idle, who created each of them, what should be fixed first, whether it was really fixed and whether the waste came back. CloudPulse reads the bill, the resource inventory, virtual machine metrics, Azure Advisor recommendations and the Activity Log on every synchronisation and turns them into one ranked list of findings, each with an owner, a status and the evidence behind it.
P|The loop has six stages, summarised in Table 1.1. Signal: on every sync CloudPulse reads the bill, inventory, metrics, recommendations and Activity Log. Context: each signal is priced per month using the actual billed cost where it exists and published list prices otherwise. Finding: the signal becomes one de-duplicated, scored task with its evidence attached. Ownership: the creator found in the Activity Log, or in the tags, is matched to a user and the finding is assigned. Action: the owner starts work, fixes, dismisses with a reason or snoozes, with notifications and a full history. Verification: CloudPulse compares seven days of billed cost before and after the fix and records the real saving. If a fixed resource comes back, the finding reopens and its saving is withdrawn. CloudPulse only reads the cloud: it never creates, changes or deletes anything in an account.

H1|1.3|OBJECTIVES AND SCOPE
P|The objectives of the project are to collect cost, inventory, usage, recommendation and audit data from an Azure subscription using a least-privilege identity; to detect unattached disks, unused public IPs, orphaned snapshots, and stopped-but-billed, idle and oversized virtual machines; to detect abnormal daily spend and forecast month-end spend and the day a budget will be exceeded; to attribute every resource to its creator and manage each finding through a role-controlled lifecycle; to count a saving only after billed cost confirms it and withdraw it if the problem returns; and to provide a reproducible Azure test environment and an automated test suite that prove these behaviours.
P|Azure is the primary supported cloud. Amazon Web Services and bills in the FinOps FOCUS open format are supported as optional inputs, so that the same findings workflow can be shown on a larger multi-provider sample bill. Notifications are in-application only, and savings are limited to resource findings: anomalies, budget risks and tagging gaps are tracked and verified but are not counted as savings, because they are investigations rather than removals. The system recommends the exact command that would fix a problem, for example deallocating a virtual machine, but it never runs that command; a person decides and acts.
H1|1.4|PROBLEM STATEMENT AND ORGANISATION OF THE REPORT
P|The problem addressed by this project can be stated in one sentence: cloud tools show how much was spent, but they do not tell a team who owns the waste, in what order to fix it and whether the fix really reduced the bill. The project therefore sets out to build a transparent, read-only platform in which each of these questions has a visible answer, and to demonstrate it on a real Azure subscription as well as on a bundled sample bill.
P|The rest of the report is organised as follows. Chapter 2 surveys the existing work on cloud cost management, anomaly detection, forecasting and cloud security. Chapter 3 describes the system: architecture, Azure credentials, data collection, detection and analytics, the finding lifecycle, the data model, security and deployment. Chapter 4 explains the test plan and the implementation challenges. Chapter 5 presents the results with screenshots of the running application. Chapter 6 concludes the report and lists future scope, followed by the abbreviations and the references.
CH|2|LITERATURE SURVEY
P|This chapter reviews the existing work that CloudPulse builds on. The survey covers the families of cost management tools that are in use today, the statistical methods used to detect unusual spend and to forecast it, the identity and access practices that make read-only monitoring of a cloud account safe, and the data formats and practices that connect these parts. The review is organised by theme rather than by year, and each theme ends with what the project takes from it.
H1|2.1|REVIEW OF EXISTING COST MANAGEMENT TOOLS AND METHODS
PL|Native cloud consoles.|Azure Cost Management, Azure Advisor and AWS Cost Explorer are the first tools most teams meet [4]. They are accurate because they read the provider's own billing records, and they are free or inexpensive. Their limits are that each covers only one provider, that recommendations are generic, and that they stop at a report: they do not record who owns a finding, whether somebody acted on it or whether the bill really changed afterwards. Azure Advisor, for example, estimates savings from reserved instances, but nothing in the console follows that recommendation until it is realised.
PL|Commercial FinOps platforms.|Commercial products add multi-cloud dashboards, allocation of spend to teams, budgets and recommendations. Their scoring methods are generally closed, so a user cannot recompute why one item ranks above another, and they usually request broad access to the account and a paid subscription. They show that cost allocation and governance are valuable, which CloudPulse adopts in a simpler, transparent form through owners and a priority score that can be recomputed by hand.
PL|Scripts and the FinOps practice.|Many teams write small scripts with the cloud command line to list unattached disks or idle addresses. Such scripts are cheap and flexible but give a one-off list with no workflow, history or verification. The FinOps Foundation frames cloud cost as shared accountability with three phases, inform, optimise and operate [3], [9]. CloudPulse maps its pages to these phases: overview and cost explorer inform, findings and priority optimise, and the lifecycle with verification operates.

PL|Anomaly detection.|Detecting unusual spend is an instance of the general anomaly detection problem surveyed by Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar [5]. For a daily cost series, robust statistics are preferred because spend is skewed and one spike must not distort the baseline. The modified z-score based on the median absolute deviation, with the cut-off of 3.5 recommended by Iglewicz and Hoaglin [6], is simple, explainable and widely used. Weekly rhythm matters, because spend on a weekday usually differs from spend at a weekend, so a good detector compares a day with the same weekday of earlier weeks, which is the approach taken in this project.
PL|Forecasting.|Exponential smoothing methods remain a strong baseline for short-horizon forecasts. Holt's linear method extends simple smoothing with a trend term [7], and Hyndman and Athanasopoulos describe how seasonal factors and prediction intervals can be added [8]. These methods need no external machine-learning library, are interpretable and can be tuned by a small grid search over their smoothing constants, which suits a tool whose numbers must be defended in front of a reader. CloudPulse uses Holt smoothing with weekday factors and an 80 percent range to forecast the month and to predict the day a budget will be passed.
PL|Identity, access and security.|Read-only monitoring is safe only if the identity it uses is narrow. The OAuth 2.0 client-credentials grant [10] lets an application obtain a short-lived access token for its own identity, and Azure role-based access control [11] lets that identity be given only the Reader and Cost Management Reader roles at subscription scope. For passwords the scrypt function [13] is a memory-hard derivation that slows guessing, and symmetric encryption of stored secrets with an authenticated scheme protects the cloud credential at rest. These practices are applied directly in the design described in Chapter 3.

H1|2.2|GAPS IN EXISTING WORK AND THE APPROACH TAKEN
P|Several challenges are reported in practice. Billing data arrives with a delay of hours, so a saving cannot be confirmed immediately. Resource tags are often missing, so the owner of a resource is unknown. Memory utilisation of virtual machines is not available without an agent, which limits right-sizing advice. Tools that request broad write permissions are risky to connect to production accounts, and many tools hide how their scores are produced [4], [9]. Taken together, the survey shows that cost visibility is widely available while owner attribution, one explainable priority and savings verified on the bill are rarely combined in one transparent, read-only tool.
P|CloudPulse addresses this gap with four decisions. It reads the owner from the Activity Log, where Azure records who created each resource, and falls back to tags. It ranks every finding with one score whose parts are stored with the finding. It verifies a saving by comparing the billed cost of the resource before and after the fix, and waits for three billed days because of the billing delay. It limits itself to read-only roles, and it explains failures in plain words so that the connection is easy to set up and to trust. The next chapter describes how these decisions are realised.
CH|3|SYSTEM FUNCTIONALITY
H1|3.1|SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
P|CloudPulse is a three-tier web application. The client tier is a single-page browser application written in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with charts drawn as SVG. The server tier is one Python process built on FastAPI; it contains the security layer, the API routers, a scheduler, the collectors for Azure, AWS and FOCUS bills, the detectors and analytics, the findings engine, the owner resolver and the data-access layer. The data tier is a SQL database, SQLite for development and PostgreSQL for production, together with a credential store in which the Azure client secret is kept as an encrypted token. Azure is an external system that is only read. Figure 3.1 shows the components and how they are connected.

H1|3.2|AZURE CREDENTIALS AND DATA COLLECTION
P|Everything CloudPulse knows about a subscription comes through one identity. An app registration with a service principal is created in the Microsoft Entra ID tenant. It has a client ID and a client secret, and it is granted the Reader and Cost Management Reader roles on the subscription only, so it cannot create, change or delete anything. CloudPulse exchanges the tenant ID, client ID and secret for a short-lived access token with the OAuth 2.0 client-credentials grant [10], and sends the token as a Bearer header with every read request. Azure Resource Manager validates the token and the role assignment before it answers. When the application runs on Azure App Service, a managed identity is used and no secret exists at all, and on a laptop the Azure CLI login can be used for a quick demonstration. Figure 3.2 shows the sequence from the one-time creation of the identity to a single read call.
F|f_cred.png|Azure credential creation, token acquisition and read-only request sequence|14
P|A client secret that an administrator types into the Connect Azure form is encrypted with Fernet, which combines AES-128 encryption with an integrity check, using a key derived from the server's secret key. Only the encrypted token is stored, the API never returns it, and the interface only shows whether it can still be read. If the connection fails, the administrator is told the exact reason: no Reader role on the subscription, a subscription that cannot be found, or a sign-in failure. The same check is available from the command line with the check-azure command, which prints Connected with the subscription name or the reason for failure.

The ARM client is a small custom class that retries on HTTP 429 and 5xx responses, waits for the Retry-After period and follows nextLink and skipToken paging. The first synchronisation back-fills 90 days of cost; later ones re-read only the last ten days because Azure revises recent days. Figure 3.3 shows the order of the steps.

H1|3.3|DETECTION AND ANALYTICS
P|The heart of the system is the analytics layer, which converts raw data into findings with plain, documented rules. Table 3.3 lists the waste rules applied to the inventory. Where Cost Management already reports the billed cost of a resource, the actual cost replaces the list-price estimate, because discounts and reservations make list prices unreliable, and the finding records which basis it used. Idle and oversized virtual machines are judged from 14 days of hourly metrics: a machine is idle when the 95th percentile of its CPU is under 5 percent and its network traffic is under 5 MB an hour, and oversized when the 95th percentile of CPU is under 20 percent and its peak is under 80 percent. Because memory cannot be measured without an agent, the oversized verdict carries a confidence that is 0.15 lower.

P|Spend anomalies use a weekday-aware robust z-score. For each billed day the baseline is the same weekday of the previous four weeks, or the previous 14 days if fewer than three such weeks exist. The day is flagged when z = (x minus median) divided by (1.4826 times MAD) is above 3.5 and the excess is at least 20 percent of the usual cost and at least one currency unit. The services that drove the increase are found by comparing each service with its own median on the baseline days. A spike is treated as ongoing when the next two days also stay high, and its impact is then projected for a month. Month-end spend is forecast with Holt's linear smoothing after the weekday pattern has been removed; the smoothing constants are chosen by a small grid search, and an 80 percent range is built from the error of the fit. A budget risk finding is raised when the forecast passes the budget, and the breach date is the first day the running total exceeds it.
P|Every finding receives a priority score so that findings of very different size can be ranked: score = 100 x (0.6 x impact score + 0.4 x severity weight) x confidence x age factor. The impact score is log10(1 + impact) divided by log10(1 + 1000), which prevents one very large item from hiding all others; the severity weight is 1.0, 0.8, 0.55 or 0.3 for critical, high, medium and low impact; the age factor raises a finding that stays open by up to 30 percent. A score of 70 or more is P1, 45 or more P2, 25 or more P3 and the rest P4, as in Table 3.4. For example, a stopped virtual machine costing $70.08 a month with confidence 0.9 scores 53, and 69 after 30 days open. Figure 3.4 plots the score against impact for the four severities.

H1|3.4|FINDING LIFECYCLE, OWNERSHIP AND VERIFICATION
P|Each finding is identified by a fingerprint, a hash of its kind and resource, so that the same problem found on consecutive syncs updates one record instead of creating many. The owner is read from the Activity Log: CloudPulse selects the earliest successful write operation on the resource, and the caller is either a user name or a GUID that belongs to an application or managed identity, which is shown as a service identity. If the log has no event, the tags owner, createdby, created-by, created_by, contact and team are tried in that order. The answer is cached, and unknown owners are retried once a day. A person matched by e-mail to a CloudPulse user receives the finding as an assignment and a notification.
P|A finding moves through the states shown in Figure 3.5. Administrators and workspace owners may perform every action, the assignee may start and resolve, and viewers can only read. Dismissing needs a written reason and snoozing is limited to 1 to 90 days. When a finding is marked resolved, the next sync checks that the resource is no longer detected, and then compares the average daily cost of the resource in the seven days before the fix with the days after it. At least three billed days must exist; the realised saving is the drop multiplied by 30.4 days. If the cost has not dropped, the finding waits up to 14 days. If a verified resource appears again, the finding is reopened and the saving is withdrawn.

H1|3.5|DATA MODEL, SECURITY AND DEPLOYMENT
P|All state is kept in one relational schema, shown in Figure 3.6. Users belong to workspaces through memberships; a workspace has cloud connections, cost snapshots, resource costs and findings; each finding has events and notifications; and sessions, the audit log, the owner cache, the price cache and the budget setting complete the model. Every query is scoped by workspace and checked against the membership of the user, so one organisation never sees another's data.
F|f_er.png|Data model of CloudPulse|15
P|Security is built into each layer, as summarised in Table 3.5. Passwords are hashed with scrypt and a random salt, sessions are server-side with a hashed token and an HttpOnly cookie, every state-changing request must carry a custom header that a foreign website cannot set, failed logins are limited to eight per fifteen minutes, and standard security headers are added to every response. The cloud identity is read-only and the stored secret is encrypted. The demonstration user is read-only and bound to the sample workspace.

H1|4.2|IMPLEMENTATION CHALLENGES
P|Four challenges shaped the implementation, listed in Table 4.3. The first was the delay of billing data. Azure publishes cost data 8 to 24 hours after usage, so a saving cannot be confirmed on the day of a fix. The solution was to verify only after three billed days, to wait up to 14 days when the cost has not yet dropped, and to use a fixed clock in the demonstration so that every figure is reproducible. Waste findings themselves do not depend on the bill, so they appear within minutes.
P|The second challenge was that resources are often created without owner tags. Reading the Activity Log solved this, because Azure records the caller of every write operation for 89 days. A caller that is a GUID belongs to an application or a managed identity and is shown as a service identity together with its application ID. For resources older than the log, only a tag can identify the owner, which is a limitation that is stated in the report. The third challenge was handling credentials safely: the identity is read-only, the secret is encrypted, a managed identity avoids the secret altogether, and a start-up message explains when no real workspace could be created because settings are missing. The fourth was keeping every number explainable, which was achieved by storing the evidence and the parts of the score with each finding.

H1|4.3|LESSONS LEARNED AND CONCLUSION
P|The work taught that billing delay must be designed for and not worked around, that audit logs are a more reliable source of ownership than tags, that least privilege and clear failure messages make a tool both safe and easy to adopt, and that visual review in a real browser catches defects that unit tests cannot. It also showed the value of keeping algorithms as small pure functions: they were easy to test, easy to explain and easy to change.
P|Testing and implementation confirm that CloudPulse detects wasted spend, attributes it to its owners and verifies savings on the bill, while addressing the challenges of delayed data, missing tags and credential safety. The results are presented in the next chapter.
CH|5|RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
P|The screenshots in this chapter were taken from the running application in its demonstration workspace. This workspace runs the same collection, detection, scoring and verification code as a live Azure workspace, on a bundled FinOps FOCUS sample bill of 1,166 daily rows from 21 March to 30 September 2024 and twelve example findings with scripted owners and history.
H1|5.1|DETECTION AND PRIORITY RESULTS
P|All 23 algorithm tests passed, and in the demonstration workspace the system produced twelve findings, listed in Table 5.1 in order of priority score and plotted in Figure 5.1. The budget risk and the large idle virtual machines outrank the small idle IP addresses, and a verified finding keeps its original score. A single explainable score lets a team decide what to fix first without arguing about units: the logarithmic impact term stops one very large item from hiding the rest, and the age factor gradually raises findings that have been forgotten.

H1|5.4|AZURE CONNECTIVITY AND DISCUSSION
P|The check-azure command and the connection test in the administrator page report either Connected with the subscription name or the exact reason for failure: no Reader role (403), subscription not found (404) or a sign-in failure. A live subscription is validated with the scripted workload and the four planted resources listed in Table 4.2. Waste findings and owners appear within minutes of a sync, while cost charts, anomalies and verification depend on Azure's billing data, which arrives 8 to 24 hours after usage; anomalies also need about a week of normal days before a spike can stand out.
P|The results support the central claim of the project. A cost problem is detected, priced, ranked, owned, acted on and checked against the bill, and the numbers on every page agree with each other. Three design choices proved important: fingerprints keep one finding per problem across syncs so that history, comments and verification stay attached; verification against the bill replaces self-reported savings; and read-only access with a managed-identity option makes the tool safe to connect, which is a precondition for adoption. Limitations were also observed: the billing delay makes verification take about a week, memory pressure of virtual machines is invisible without an agent, owners are known only for the last 89 days unless tags exist, and the scheduler and login limiter keep state in one process, so this version targets a single instance.
CH|6|CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE
H1|6.1|CONCLUSION
P|In conclusion, CloudPulse proves to be a valuable and practical platform for controlling cloud spend. It reads an Azure subscription with a least-privilege, read-only identity, detects idle and wasted resources, flags anomalies and forecasts budget breaches, assigns every finding to the person who created the resource and counts a saving only after the bill confirms it. Transparent algorithms make every figure explainable, and 27 automated tests with a scripted Azure environment support the results. In the demonstration workspace the system reported $262.94 a month of open waste and $33.44 a month of verified savings at 94 percent estimate accuracy.
P|The project also shows that a small, understandable code base can deliver what many closed tools do not: an owner for every finding, one score that can be recomputed by hand, and a saving that is counted only when the bill proves it. The platform aligns with the growing emphasis on FinOps and with the responsible use of cloud resources, since idle infrastructure also consumes energy in data centres.
